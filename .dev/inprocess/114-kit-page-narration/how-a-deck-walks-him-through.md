# A deck explains before it asks, and argues in pictures

Tim, 11 November, on version 1.0.2 of the architecture repair brief: a slide with only text and narration is inadequate for explaining these complex decisions; the deck needs visuals and must walk him through the problem and each decision, one at a time, with each decision slide after its explanation. He did not understand why the decision slides were on slide two or what came after them, and he stopped watching.

## What version 1.0.2 actually did

Twelve slides. The two votes were slides 2 and 3, before any explanation. Three slides carried a picture — `why-not-here.svg` on slide 4, `signature-gap.svg` on slide 5, `after-you-answer.svg` on slide 11. The other nine were a heading, prose and narration. Four more pictures existed only as option icons inside the two votes.

So he was asked both questions before being told what either was about, and two thirds of the deck had nothing to look at.

## Where the mistake came from

His standing rule for messages is to lead with the decision and never bury it. That rule is about a written message he reads and skims, where he decides what to read. A narrated deck is played to him in order and he cannot skip ahead, so leading with the vote asks the question before the argument exists. Applying the message rule to a deck produced the opposite of what it is for.

## The rule for every deck sent to him

1. Every slide carries a picture that makes that slide's argument. A heading, prose and narration is not a slide. A slide with nothing worth drawing has nothing worth a slide and folds into its neighbour.
2. He can follow the argument from the pictures with the sound off, and from the narration with his eyes shut. Neither carries it alone.
3. A vote comes immediately after the slides that set it up, and never before them. One choice is explained and asked, then the next is explained and asked.
4. A picture draws the real thing with its real names, never an invented shape that illustrates the idea.
5. Colour never carries a meaning on its own; the label carries it.

## Version 1.0.3

Fourteen slides, a picture on each. Slides 1 to 6 build the problem from where it ends up, through what a beat can reach today, what the page carries, pointing at one card, pointing at one row, and the chain of consequence a language change runs through. Slide 7 asks `member-identity`. Slides 8 to 10 build the second problem: how a target is proven, where it is proven today, and what closing this repository's own gate changes. Slide 11 asks `selector-proof`. Slides 12 to 14 are what his answers release, what is deliberately not asked here, and the record.
